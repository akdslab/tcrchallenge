A grand challenge in immunology

Adaptive immunity is the part of immune defense that responds to specific targets and can remember previous encounters. T cells are immune cells central to this system. Some help coordinate immune responses; others can destroy infected or cancerous cells. Proteins on their surface, called T-cell receptors (TCRs), recognize molecular targets displayed by other cells.

Predicting which peptide–MHC targets a T-cell receptor recognizes is an unsolved grand challenge in immunology. Sequencing tells us which receptors are present; it does not, by itself, tell us what they recognize. The aim is to connect receptor sequences to their targets and predict recognition across combinations that have not yet been tested. TCR Challenge takes its name from this scientific problem.

How a protein becomes a T-cell target

Cells break down proteins into short fragments called peptides. Some of these peptides are held by MHC molecules and displayed at the cell surface. A peptide can come from a cell’s own proteins, an infection, or material taken up from its surroundings.

A T-cell receptor encounters the peptide together with the MHC molecule holding it. This combination is peptide–MHC, or pMHC. In humans, MHC molecules are called HLA. The peptide and its HLA context both belong in the description of a target.

MHC class I generally presents peptides to CD8 T cells, which can kill infected or cancerous cells. MHC class II generally presents peptides to CD4 T cells, which help coordinate immune responses. Both classes matter to human disease; the sequence tutorials here use HLA class I examples.

A receptor has a sequence and a shape

This resource focuses on alpha–beta T-cell receptors, made from an alpha chain and a beta chain. As T cells develop, they assemble receptor genes by joining DNA segments and adding or removing DNA at the joins. This process produces a wide range of receptor sequences within each person.

Variable loops on both chains contact the peptide–MHC surface. The CDR3 loops span these gene-segment joins and are among the most variable parts of the receptor. Their sequences are important inputs to our models.

Recognition in health and disease

Cancer: recognizing a tumor

Cancer cells can display protein fragments, or peptides, on MHC molecules at their surface. Some of these peptides come from proteins altered by tumor mutations and distinguish the cancer from healthy tissue. When a T-cell receptor recognizes one of these peptide–MHC targets, it can help direct a response against the cancer.

Researchers seek receptors that recognize tumor targets while sparing healthy tissue. Identifying these receptor–target pairs is part of developing T-cell therapies. Recognition alone does not ensure that a tumor will be eliminated: cancer cells can suppress the immune response.

Autoimmunity: when recognition harms tissue

Healthy cells display fragments of their own proteins as peptides held by MHC molecules. T cells that recognize these targets are called self-reactive. The immune system normally limits harmful responses through tolerance: some self-reactive T cells are removed during development; others are kept from causing damage by controls elsewhere in the body.

When these controls are insufficient, self-reactive T cells can damage tissue or help other immune cells do so. Identifying their peptide–MHC targets helps researchers study how harmful responses arise and persist. A self-reactive receptor alone is not evidence of disease; such receptors also occur in healthy people.

Infection: recognizing an infected cell

During an infection, cells can display protein fragments from a virus or another pathogen as peptides held by MHC molecules. A cytotoxic, or killer, T cell can recognize one of these peptide–MHC targets and kill the infected cell. Helper T cells recognize peptide–MHC on cells that present material from the infection and help coordinate other immune defenses.

Mapping these receptor–target pairs helps researchers identify which parts of a pathogen T cells respond to. Some responding T cells persist as memory cells, helping the body respond to a later encounter.

Why recognition is difficult to predict

Limited paired data

The challenge is to predict recognition beyond the pairs already measured. Receptor sequencing provides many sequences, while experimental data that links each receptor to its targets remains limited. Models need to learn from these incomplete observations and be tested on new receptors, targets and combinations.

Cross-reactivity

Recognition is selective, but a receptor is not limited to one peptide. Experiments have shown that a single TCR can recognize many different peptides, a property called cross-reactivity. Finding one target is therefore only part of the problem. We also need to understand what else that receptor may recognize.

Peptide and HLA context

The possible combinations of receptors, peptides and HLA molecules are too numerous to test exhaustively. HLA adds another source of variation. Different HLA molecules present different sets of peptides, and the receptor contacts the combined surface. Peptide binding to MHC and TCR binding to peptide–MHC are distinct prediction tasks.

Testing new receptors and targets

Evaluation needs to ask what is new to the model: the receptor, the peptide, or their combination. Ranking candidates for a receptor represented in training differs from predicting targets for a receptor excluded from training. The benchmarks describe these differences so that readers can judge which evidence applies to their experiment.

Binding and T-cell activation

Binding is also only one part of a T-cell response. Other signals and the state of the cell influence activation. A binding score or a predicted structure helps form an experimental hypothesis; establishing a functional response requires a measurement.

Learning from protein sequences

We have far more protein sequences than experimentally determined structures. These sequences carry a record of evolution: changes that preserve a protein’s structure and function are more likely to persist. Protein language models are AI systems that learn patterns in this large body of sequence data.

ESMC base learns to predict hidden amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, from the surrounding sequence. In doing so, it develops numerical representations that capture information about protein structure and function. ESMFold2 base uses ESMC representations to predict three-dimensional structures. This gives us a starting point for modeling proteins whose structures have not been measured.

Fine-tuning continues training on data relevant to a particular task. We fine-tune these open models on T-cell recognition data to score receptor–target pairs, propose sequences for experiments, and predict how a receptor and its target may fit together. Their performance must be tested on receptor–target combinations excluded from training.

Why use single-sequence structure prediction?

Many structure-prediction methods compare related proteins in a multiple sequence alignment (MSA). Positions that tend to change together across related sequences can offer clues to how a protein folds or how two proteins interact. TCRs pose a particular difficulty: their most variable regions are assembled within each person, and a specific receptor–peptide pair generally lacks a family of matching pairs across species to compare.

Alignments can still provide useful information about the individual proteins. The DecoderTCR-Fold workflow uses one sequence for each component of the complex, without requiring an MSA. It draws on patterns learned during protein-model training and on fine-tuning for TCR–pMHC interactions.

Building the data with CLASP-seq

Many combinations of T-cell receptors, peptides and MHC molecules remain unmeasured. Alongside open models and datasets, we are developing an experimental protocol so more labs can contribute measurements to help test and improve predictions.

CLASP-seq is a protocol in development that brings two engineered cell populations together: one carries T-cell receptors, and the other displays peptide–MHC targets. After pairing and sorting, 10x capture and targeted sequencing recover receptor and target identities. Their recovery together provides evidence to evaluate with controls; it does not by itself establish specific recognition.

Measurements from different labs can broaden the receptors, targets and experimental settings represented in the data. Share assay conditions and controls with each dataset so others can interpret it, and keep model evaluation data separate from training data.

Questions that come up in practice

What information do I need to make a prediction?

For a TCR–pMHC pair, you need the receptor’s alpha and beta chain sequences, the peptide sequence and its HLA context. Some workflows reconstruct receptor sequences from gene names and CDR3 sequences. The tutorials show the required fields and example files. Check the model documentation for supported HLA alleles and input types.

Which model should I use?

Use DecoderTCR to score sequences, screen a repertoire against a peptide list, and inspect peptide profiles. DecoderTCR-Fold is a folding model for TCR–pMHC structure prediction and interaction scoring.

Will a model work on unfamiliar receptors or targets?

Models often perform better on examples similar to their training data and less well on unfamiliar ones. Similarity alone does not guarantee a reliable prediction.

Testing new combinations of familiar partners is different from testing new receptors or targets. The benchmarks explain what was used in training and what was held out, so you can judge which results apply to your experiment.

Does a high score mean that a T cell will respond?

Not by itself. A score can help select receptor–target pairs to test, and a predicted structure suggests how a complex might fit together. Neither establishes binding or a cellular response. Activation also depends on how the target is presented, other signals and the cell’s state.

The models have different score definitions. See the sequence-score guide and DecoderTCR-Fold overview for their respective tasks.
